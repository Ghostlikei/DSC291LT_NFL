Batch Stochastic No-Free-Lunch

Week — No-Free-Lunch Theorems

DSC 190/291 Learning Theory  ·  Tianhao Wang  ·  UCSD Spring 2026

1. Setting

In the batch stochastic setting, a learner receives a random i.i.d. sample of labelled examples and outputs a hypothesis. The Batch Stochastic NFL theorem says that for any learner, there exists a realizable distribution on which the learner fails with constant probability.

Definition 1 (Domain and binary classification). Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a finite measurable space. A target function is any map $f : \mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}$. A distribution $D$ on $\mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\}$ is realizable if there exists $f$ with $D\bigl(\{(x, f(x))\}\bigr) = 1$, i.e., all labels are correct under $f$.
Definition 2 (Deterministic learner). A deterministic learner is a function $$A : \bigl(\mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\}\bigr)^* \;\longrightarrow\; (\mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}).$$ Given a training sequence $S \in (\mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\})^m$, $A$ outputs a hypothesis $A(S) : \mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}$.
Definition 3 (Risk and failure probability). The risk of hypothesis $h$ under distribution $D$ is $$R(D, h) \;:=\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}\bigl[\mathbf{1}[h(x) \neq y]\bigr].$$ The failure probability of learner $A$ on $m$ i.i.d. samples from $D$ is $$\delta(D, A, m) \;:=\; \Pr_{S \sim D^m}\bigl[R(D,\, A(S)) \geq \tfrac{1}{8}\bigr].$$

2. Main Theorem

Theorem 1 (Batch Stochastic No-Free-Lunch). Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a finite domain with $|\mathcal{X}| \geq 2m$. For any deterministic learner $A$, there exists a realizable distribution $D$ on $\mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\}$ such that $$\delta(D,\, A,\, m) \;\geq\; \frac{1}{7}.$$ That is, with probability at least $1/7$ over $m$ i.i.d. samples from $D$, the learner's hypothesis has risk at least $1/8$.
Takeaway. No matter how clever the learner, there is always a realizable distribution for which it fails (risk $\geq 1/8$) on at least $1/7$ of all training samples of size $m$.

3. Proof

Case 1: zero samples ($m = 0$)

Base case. With no training data, $A([])$ is a fixed hypothesis. Let $x_0 \in \mathcal{X}$ be any point and set $b := \lnot A([])(x_0)$. Define $D := \mathrm{Dirac}(x_0, b)$ — the Dirac measure at the single labelled point $(x_0, b)$.
  • $D$ is realizable via $f(x_0) = b$ (extended arbitrarily).
  • The unique 0-sample "training set" is the empty list, so $A([])$ predicts $\lnot b$ at $x_0$, giving risk $= 1 \geq 1/8$.
  • The $D^0$ measure of the failure event is 1 (the only sample is the empty sequence), so $\delta(D, A, 0) = 1 \geq 1/7$.
□

Case 2: positive samples ($m \geq 1$)

The positive-sample case uses a four-step argument: pick a support, prove a fixed-sample lower bound, derive a contradiction via double-counting, and bridge from the combinatorial setting to the measure-theoretic one.

Step 1: Pick a support $C$ of size $2m$

Since $2m \leq |\mathcal{X}|$, choose a finset $C \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ with $|C| = 2m$. For any labelling $f : C \to \{0,1\}$, define $D_f$ to be the uniform distribution on the labelled graph of $f$ over $C$: $$D_f := \mathrm{Uniform}\bigl(\{(x, f(x)) : x \in C\}\bigr).$$ The distribution $D_f$ is realizable (by construction, $f$ has risk 0 under $D_f$).

Step 2: Fixed-sample lower bound

Lemma 2 (Average risk $\geq \frac{|\mathcal{F}|}{4}$). Let $\mathcal{F} = \{0,1\}^C$ be all labellings and $\Omega = (C)^m$ be all samples. For any sample $S \in \Omega$, $$\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} R(D_f,\, A(\mathrm{sample}(S, f))) \;\geq\; \frac{1}{4}.$$
Proof sketch. The sample $S = (z_1, \ldots, z_m)$ queries only $m$ of the $2m$ points in $C$. Let $U$ be the set of unseen points: $|U| \geq m$ (by a cardinality argument). For any unseen point $z \in U$, the label $f(z)$ is invisible to $A$, so pairing each labelling $f$ with its flip $f^z$ at $z$ shows that the two labels contribute total risk 1 at $z$. Summing over $m$ unseen points and dividing by $|C| = 2m$ gives average risk $\geq m/(2m) = 1/2$ per consistent pair, hence average risk $\geq 1/4$ overall.
□

Step 3: Contradiction by double-counting

Suppose, for contradiction, that for every labelling $f \in \mathcal{F}$, the failure rate satisfies $$\delta_f \;:=\; \frac{|\{S \in \Omega : R(D_f, A(S)) \geq 1/8\}|}{|\Omega|} < \frac{1}{7}.$$ Consider the double sum $$\Sigma := \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}||\Omega|} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \sum_{S \in \Omega} R(D_f, A(S)).$$

  • Upper bound (from failure-rate hypothesis): For each $(f, S)$ pair, $R(D_f, A(S)) \leq 1$ always. On "bad" samples ($R \geq 1/8$) use bound 1; on "good" samples use bound $1/8 - \varepsilon$: $$\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_S R(D_f, A(S)) \leq \delta_f \cdot 1 + (1 - \delta_f) \cdot 1 < \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{8} = \frac{15}{56} < \frac{1}{4}.$$ Averaging over $f$ gives $\Sigma < 1/4$.
  • Lower bound (from Lemma 2): Swapping the order of summation (Fubini) and applying Lemma 2 to each $S$ gives $\Sigma \geq 1/4$.

These two bounds contradict each other, so some $f \in \mathcal{F}$ must have $\delta_f \geq 1/7$.

Step 4: Bridge to the measure-theoretic setting

Lemma 3 (Measure bridge). The failure rate $\delta_f$ computed combinatorially (as a proportion of uniform samples from $C^m$) is bounded above by the measure-theoretic failure probability $\delta(D_f, A, m)$.

The proof uses the fact that the product measure $D_f^m$, when restricted to the event that all $m$ samples land in the support-indexed set, assigns exactly the same probability as the uniform combinatorial distribution over $\Omega = C^m$. Bad combinatorial samples embed injectively into the $D_f^m$-failure event, and measure is preserved under this embedding.

Conclusion. From Step 3, pick $f^* \in \mathcal{F}$ with $\delta_{f^*} \geq 1/7$. By Lemma 3, $\delta(D_{f^*}, A, m) \geq \delta_{f^*} \geq 1/7$. Set $D = D_{f^*}$; this distribution is realizable, and $\delta(D, A, m) \geq 1/7$.
□

4. Proof Summary

Proof structure at a glance
  1. $m = 0$: Dirac at a single point labelled adversarially.
  2. $m \geq 1$:
    1. Pick support $C \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ with $|C| = 2m$.
    2. For each $f : C \to \{0,1\}$, define $D_f = $ uniform on graph of $f$.
    3. Lower bound: average risk over all $(f, S)$ pairs $\geq 1/4$ (unseen points contribute independent label pairs).
    4. Contradiction: if every $\delta_f < 1/7$, then average risk $< 1/4$ (piecewise upper bound + Fubini), contradiction.
    5. Bridge: combinatorial failure rate $\leq$ measure-theoretic failure probability.
Remark (Why $1/7$ and $1/8$?). The constants are not tight. The bound $\delta_f < 1/7$ combined with the piecewise risk bound $\leq 1/8$ on good samples yields average risk $< 1/7 \cdot 1 + 6/7 \cdot 1/8 = 2/14 + 6/56 < 1/4$. The exact constants are chosen to make the arithmetic work; tighter analysis gives stronger bounds.
Remark (Why $|C| = 2m$?). We need at least $m$ unseen points in a sample of size $m$ from $C$. With $|C| = 2m$ and a sample of size $m$, the pigeonhole principle guarantees at least $m$ unseen points (the complement of the seen set has size $\geq m$).

5. Formal Statement in Lean 4

The full machine-verified proof is in NFL/claude_full/BatchStochastic.lean. Below are the core definitions and the main theorem statement. 

-- A deterministic learning algorithm
abbrev Learner (X : Type*) := List (X × Bool) → (X → Bool)

-- 0-1 loss on a single labelled example
def zeroOneLoss (h : X → Bool) (xy : X × Bool) : ℝ :=
  if h xy.1 = xy.2 then 0 else 1

-- Measure-theoretic risk
noncomputable def risk (D : Measure (X × Bool)) (h : X → Bool) : ℝ :=
  ∫ xy, zeroOneLoss h xy ∂D

-- Failure probability over m i.i.d. samples
noncomputable def failureProb
    (D : Measure (X × Bool)) (A : Learner X) (m : Nat) (ε : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  ((Measure.pi (fun _ : Fin m => D))
    {S : Fin m → (X × Bool) | ε ≤ risk D (A (List.ofFn S))}).toReal

-- Main theorem
theorem batch_stochastic_NFL
    [Fintype X] [MeasurableSingletonClass X] [Nonempty X]
    (A : Learner X) (m : Nat) (hm : 2 * m ≤ Fintype.card X) :
    ∃ D : Measure (X × Bool), IsProbabilityMeasure D ∧
      (∃ f : X → Bool, risk D f = 0) ∧
      (1 : ℝ) / 7 ≤ failureProb D A m (1 / 8) := by
  -- m = 0 case: Dirac at a fooling point
  -- m ≥ 1 case: support C of size 2m, double-counting contradiction
  cases Nat.eq_zero_or_pos m with
  | inl hm0 => exact batch_stochastic_NFL_zero A
  | inr hm_pos =>
      obtain ⟨C, hCcard⟩ := exists_support_card m hm
      -- exists f* with combinatorial failure rate ≥ 1/7
      obtain ⟨fZ, hfZ⟩ := exists_abstractFailureRate_ge C hCcard A m hm_pos
      -- bridge: fZ induces a realizable measure with failure prob ≥ 1/7
      exact ⟨(graphPMF C fZ ⟨..⟩).toMeasure, inferInstance, ⟨fZ, ..⟩, ..⟩

6. Further Discussion

Comparison with the online adversarial setting

In the online adversarial theorem, the adversary picks the target after seeing the learner's prediction. Here, the target $f$ is fixed before sampling begins — it is an existential claim, not a minimax one. The probabilistic difficulty comes not from an adaptive adversary but from the inherent information-theoretic limitation of seeing only $m$ of the $2m$ points.

The role of the double-counting argument

The core of the proof is a Fubini-style interchange: $$\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_f \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_S R_f(S) = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_S \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_f R_f(S) \geq \frac{1}{4}.$$ If every $f$ had small failure rate, the left side would be $< 1/4$, contradicting the right side. This is essentially a probabilistic pigeonhole argument.

Measure-theoretic bridge

The combinatorial argument works over the finite set $\Omega = C^m$, but the theorem's conclusion is measure-theoretic (probability under $D^m$). The bridge lemma shows that the uniform distribution over the support-indexed sample space $\Omega$ is exactly the pushforward of $D_f^m$ along the projection to $C$. This requires careful use of product-measure decompositions and the fact that the bad-sample set embeds injectively into the $D_f^m$-failure event.

Key insight. The unseen half of the support $C$ provides information-theoretically independent labels that no learner can predict, regardless of how it processes the seen half. This inherent blindspot forces a constant fraction of samples to yield bad hypotheses.
